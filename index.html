Before the Story  ·  One Molecule

Many Plants, One Blue

Indigo is one molecule — indigotin — made by plants in at least four unrelated families: legumes in South Asia, Africa and the Americas, a cabbage relative in Europe, a knotweed in East Asia and an acanthus in the Himalaya. Cultures that never met found their own plant and arrived at the same chemistry.

“Blue is taken from the indigo plant, yet it is bluer than the plant.” 靑，取之於藍而靑於藍

Xunzi, “Encouraging Learning”, 3rd century BCE · Wikisource (Chinese)
From Leaf to Cloth — Why Indigo Is Hard

Indigo is not in the leaf as a blue, and it does not dissolve in water. Every dyer on every continent solved the same two problems: make the blue, then make it soluble long enough to get it into a fibre.

Plantation Blue  ·  1650 – 1789

The Atlantic Supply, in Pounds

Between the 1650s and the 1790s most of Europe's indigo came from American plantations: French Saint-Domingue, Spanish Guatemala and San Salvador, British South Carolina and Georgia. These are their surviving records, each in the unit its source used.

South Carolina & Georgia Indigo Exports, 1747–1788 (lb)

Exports took off on a provincial bounty in 1747, Parliament's sixpence-a-pound bounty from 1749, and enslaved labour. The tallest bar, 1775, covers only the 6½ months to 24 February. Gaps are years with no figure in the series: 1767, and the Revolution — the bounty lapsed in 1777 and exports resumed only after 1783.

Three Empires' Indigo, Compared

Saint-Domingue shipped more indigo to France than Carolina sent to Britain — over four million livres in 1771–72. Central American points mix harvests, exports and period averages (dashed line), so read them as orders of magnitude. French livres are converted to pounds at ×1.079.

Indigo Gives Way to Sugar

Indigo works in the Saint-Domingue parish of L'Arcahaye: 186 in 1752, 23 by 1775, after irrigation turned the plain to sugar. Indigo was a crop for newcomers and smaller planters; sugar followed the capital. The 1789 figure is an estimate.

Empire Blue  ·  1790 – 1896

Bengal, Bihar and the Peak of Plant Indigo

When the Atlantic supply failed, the East India Company rebuilt Indian indigo in Bengal; after Bengal's ryots revolted, the planters moved to Bihar. By the 1890s British India grew indigo on 1.7 million acres. Then the chemists arrived.

India's Indigo, 1795–1924 (tonnes)

Two official series on one axis, both converted to tonnes: Bengal's production in factory maunds (1795–1831) and British India's exports in hundredweights (1876–1924). They measure different things — output and exports — but together they show the arc: about 5,300 t made in 1825–26, a record 9,517 t exported in 1895–96, and about 170 t by 1924–25.

Area Under Indigo, British India, 1890–1925 (acres)

From 1,705,977 acres in 1894–95 to 146,000 in 1914–15. The late spike is the First World War: with German supplies cut off the area rose to 765,000 acres in 1916–17, led by Madras rather than Bihar, then fell back to 107,000 by 1924–25. Official returns, fiscal years.

Synthetic Shock  ·  1897 – 1945

How a Laboratory Ended a Plantation Crop

BASF's Indigo Pure went on sale on 10 July 1897. By 1905 Britain was spending more on synthetic than on natural indigo, and by 1914 natural manufacture had practically ceased everywhere except Bihar.

Britain's Indigo Bill: Natural vs Synthetic (£)

UK imports by value. Natural indigo fell from £1.53 million in 1896 to £137,000 in 1908. Synthetic imports, recorded from 1902, overtook it in 1905 — at a fraction of the old bill, because the price had collapsed too.

Both Prices Fell; Synthetic Fell Faster

Index, 1897 = 100. India's average export value per hundredweight (to 1904–05) against BASF's price per kilogram (to 1914). By 1914 BASF's price was less than half its 1897 level. Both lines are computed from the cited figures (DERIVED).

Japan's Awa Indigo, Tokushima Prefecture, 1903–1991 (hectares, log scale)

The collapse was not only Indian. Tokushima grew about 15,000 ha of indigo in 1903; imported Indian and then synthetic indigo cut it to 7,542 ha by 1907, 502 by 1926 and 4 by 1965. A heritage craft has since held it near twenty.

Denim & Revival  ·  1946 – today

A Dye for One Garment

Almost all indigo now goes into one product. Published estimates of world output disagree by nearly half and no primary industry statistic exists; what has been measured carefully is the footprint of a single pair of jeans.

World Synthetic Indigo: Published Estimates (t/yr)

14,000 t consumed in 1995 by the reckoning of BASF's indigo chief, then estimates from 50,000 to 88,000 t a year in the 2010s and 2020s. Lighter bars are estimates. A 2024 market-research figure of about 97,000 t could not be traced to a readable source and is not shown.

Where One Pair of 501s Uses Its Water (litres)

Levi Strauss & Co.'s 2015 life-cycle assessment: 3,781 litres over a pair's life, two-thirds of it to grow the cotton; dyeing sits inside fabric production. UNEP puts an average pair at 3,000–4,000 litres and UN News has cited 7,500 — different boundaries, not comparable.

The Human Cost

Who Grew the Blue

The trade's volumes were made by coerced hands. Guatemala's fermentation vats were deadly work; the plantations of Saint-Domingue and Carolina ran on enslaved Africans, bought in part with indigo-dyed Indian cloth; Bengal's ryots were bound by debt and Bihar's tenants by contract. Synthesis moved the costs rather than ending them.

“…it is in no instance the result of free agency, but that it is always compulsory.”

Ashley Eden, magistrate, on indigo cultivation in Bengal — evidence to the Indigo Commission, 1860 · Minutes of Evidence, Internet Archive

About

What This Is, and How to Read It

An open-data autopsy of one dye across six thousand years — where it came from, how it spread, what it cost, and who paid.

Indigo is the only colour that has been a world commodity for its whole history. This project follows one molecule from the oldest dated indigo-dyed cloth to the denim mill, with a world map, a set of charts, a year scrubber and a rights-cleared picture archive — and it shows its working for every number on the page.

It is a domain fork of Downtown Tampa: A Civic Development Autopsy, itself a fork of The 2026 Iran War: A Geoeconomic Autopsy: same architecture, same citation standard, a different subject — and a planet-sized study area. The site is static: no build step for the reader, no backend. Everything it knows lives in one cited data file, compiled from the research notes in the repository.

How the evidence is graded

Every figure carries a source object naming the institution, the document, the date and a status, and most carry the exact sentence from the source that supports them. Nothing is presented as fact because it sounds right.

  • Confirmed The cited page states exactly this. Hero and footer numbers must be confirmed.
  • Pending The source is the right one or a credible secondary account, but the figure has not yet been read in the primary record. Treat as provisional.
  • Derived Computed or editorial: a unit conversion, an index, a remainder, an era boundary, a sankey model. The note names the inputs. Do not cite these as primary figures.

The Source Audit prints the live tally. Where sources disagree — the date of Maya Blue's first use, the depth of the blue layer in the Sacred Cenote, three incompatible figures for the water in a pair of jeans — the disagreement is printed, not averaged.

Myths this project could not confirm

Several of indigo's best-known stories did not survive the research pass. The 1577 imperial ban on a "devil's dye" was aimed at a vitriol-based colour, not indigo; the German bans on indigo itself date from 1650 and 1654. No primary text was found for Henri IV's reported death penalty for using indigo; the documented French ban is from 1598. England's Elizabethan dye act of 1581 targeted logwood and names woad-and-indigo as lawful. Eliza Lucas did not create Carolina's industry single-handed. And "Japan Blue" as a nineteenth-century coinage could not be traced to a primary text. Each is noted where it would otherwise appear.

What is still missing

There is no trustworthy modern production statistic: published estimates of world synthetic indigo output range from 50,000 to 88,000 tonnes a year, and China's share can only be stated qualitatively. Britain's eighteenth-century imports by origin, the East India Company's early indigo volumes and an annual Guatemalan export series are named gaps. The research brief for closing them is in the repository.

The study area

The world, from the earliest dated indigo-dyed textile to the present. The subject is the colorant indigotin — whichever plant or factory it came from — and the trade, labour and chemistry built around it. Other blues (Egyptian blue, lapis, Prussian blue) appear only where they meet indigo's story.
